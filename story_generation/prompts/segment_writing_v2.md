## 0. How to read these rules

These are **tendencies and tests, not quotas.** Where a number appears it
is a rough aim, and a deliberate exception that serves the story is always
better than mechanical compliance.

Two failure modes matter equally:

- **Padding** — words added because a length was expected. Always a failure.
- **Formula** — a rule applied so evenly that the shape shows through. If
  every segment opens with dialogue, or every description reaches for a
  food comparison, the writing has become a template. Vary it. Sameness
  across a shelf of stories is as bad as weakness in one.

## 1. What you are writing

A story a child of **this story's age** hears aloud — read by an adult or
spoken by a voice. Not a story they read. Not a description of a place. A
thing that happens, to us, with someone we like, that keeps going.

The test for any sentence: **would a child this age lean in, or drift?**
Children drift through description that does nothing. They lean in for
action, for voices, for things that touch their own body — and for a
beautiful image that makes them feel something, which is not the same as
decoration.

## 2. Age shapes almost everything

| Age band | Sentences | Repetition | Voices vs. narration | One path |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3-4 | mostly very short, one idea each | loved; refrains and repeated phrases carry the whole story | simple exchanges; keep narration minimal | ~350 words |
| 5-6 | short and medium, an occasional long one | still loved; a phrase that comes back is a gift | dialogue and action carry roughly half or more | ~700 words |
| 7-8 | fuller range; clauses fine | use as callback rather than refrain | narration can carry more; dialogue still frequent | ~1,200 words |
| 9-10 | full range, longer flows welcome | only as a deliberate motif | narration can lead; dialogue where it lands hardest | ~1,700 words |
| 11-12 | full adult range; subordinate clauses, a long periodic sentence when it earns it | a returning line only if the story is built on one | narration leads; dialogue is chosen, not constant | ~2,200 words |

Word counts are **ceilings, not targets** (see §8). Everything else in this
table is a lean, not a law.

## 2b. Register climbs with age — the oldest band is not the youngest band louder

The single thing that most often makes an older story feel wrong is a
narrator using a younger register: naming feelings, decorating with
sound-words, chanting a refrain, explaining the obvious. An 11-year-old
hears that as being talked down to and closes the book.

- **Sound-words (onomatopoeia) must carry information, not texture.** A
  sound belongs when it *is* the thing — a steam engine whose "chug" is the
  physics, a robot whose beep is its only speech, sonar that pings back a
  shape. That is fine at any age. A narrator sprinkling "clank-clank-whir"
  for atmosphere is decoration, and by 9-12 it reads as childish. Ask of
  every sound-word: does the story lose a fact if I cut it? If not, cut it.
- **Feelings are shown, never named, from 7 up** (see §11). By 11-12 even a
  shown feeling is usually better left for the child to infer from what a
  character does.
- **Refrains and repeated kid-shaped speech are a 3-6 pleasure.** At 9-12 a
  line may return once if the whole story is built on it; otherwise it reads
  as padding.
- **Trust the older listener's patience.** A 11-12 story can hold a longer
  arc, a quieter beat, a fact that takes three sentences to land. It does
  not need a jolt every paragraph. What it must not do is stall.

The register a band wants is a lean, like everything in §2 — a deep-dive
robotics story for a nine-year-old who loves machines can run warmer and
sound-richer than this suggests. Judge by the listener, not the number.

## 3. How a story opens — the first stretch

Do not begin mid-action at a threshold. Before anything is opened, entered
or discovered, the opening should give the listener three things quickly:

1. **Who we are** — us, together, plainly.
2. **Where we are** — somewhere the child can picture standing right now;
   ordinary before it turns strange.
3. **The pull** — the thing that makes us go: a sound, a smell, a movement.
   Best when it arrives *at us*.

A door with no reason to be opened is a stage set, not an opening.

Opening on a spoken line or a question often works beautifully — but not
every time, or every story starts to sound the same.

## 4. Voices and action usually carry more than narration

- **The companion should mostly be doing something**, not standing nearby
  being funny: if we listen, they often listen too; if we are stuck, they
  try something. They can have quiet beats — a guide who is silent at the
  right moment is powerful — but a companion who only decorates is a waste.
- **Younger children respond to short, repeated, kid-shaped speech**
  ("Listen. Listen." "You came! You came!"). Reach for that often at 3-6,
  less at 7-8, rarely at 9-10 — and not in every line at any age, or it
  turns into a tic.
- **We can speak too.** Curiosity is stronger heard than narrated: "What is
  that?" we ask. Use it when it earns its place.
- As a rough aim, at 5-6 about half the segment carries speech or physical
  action. Treat that as a smell test, not a measurement.

## 5. Description — four ways to earn its place

A descriptive phrase belongs when it does at least one of these:

- **invite** — it reaches toward us: "a tunnel waits for us"
- **involve** — we feel it in the body: "the ground hums under our knees"
- **advance** — it is used later: the seed, the ledge, the crystal window
- **ignite** — it stirs a feeling, or makes a piece of real knowledge land:
  a beautiful image is worth its words even when it does nothing else

That fourth one matters. Beauty is not decoration. **The real failure is
never "a description existed" — it is a description that was added to fill
space, or one written weakly.**

On weak writing specifically: "the tunnel glows orange" is fine — it is
true and a child can see it. "like someone left a nightlight on inside the
whole mountain" is the problem: a limp, abstract comparison that adds
length without adding sight. When an image is worth having, make it
*specific*; when it is not, let the plain true thing stand alone.

**Usual suspects when a segment feels padded** — check these first, cut
what is not pulling weight:

| Suspect | Why it usually goes |
|---|---|
| where a character came from ("out from under a rock ledge pops…") | nobody in the story cares; introduce them by what they do or say to us |
| a second and third image piled onto a static scene | one strong image on the action beats three on scenery |
| scenery introduced and never used again | a promise the story breaks |
| narrating travel between places | arriving is the interesting part |
| restating what just happened | the child was there |
| abstract mood words doing the work: cozy, magical, wondrous, serene | usually a sign of telling a feeling instead of giving one; say what the body feels |
| a second comic bit in the same beat | one usually lands better than three |

## 6. Images from the child's own day

Compare the strange thing to something this age has touched, eaten or slept
in: bread in the oven, thick tomato soup on the stove, dough rising, a cat
purring, bathwater, flour on a counter. Food and kitchen images are
especially strong — children are always interested in food.

Sometimes a small **list** of concrete delights is exactly right ("thick as
tomato soup, as porridge, as the jam at the bottom of the jar") — a run of
three can be a pleasure at any age. Sometimes one clean image is stronger.
Judge per beat; do not do either every time.

Avoid comparisons to things a child has not met, and avoid stacking a
simile onto something already vivid.

## 6b. Warmth and company

Distilled from an expert's line-by-line revision of a pilot story. These
are the moves that make a story feel *fond* rather than merely well made.
Use them sparingly — one or two per segment at most, never all five.

- **The world answers, in company.** The big thing is not only observed; it
  responds a little. "The mountain rumbles back, not in answer but in
  company." Nature as a companion rather than a spectacle. (Keep the phrase
  plain — see the caution below.)
- **The companion is felt, not just seen.** One physical point of contact
  makes a character dear: a tiny heartbeat against our ear at the wall,
  claws tickling through a shirt, a warm shoulder. Physical touch beats any
  amount of describing how nice they are.
- **Gentle humour from the grand thing being, in some way, ordinary.** "A
  pot that knows it has all day." "A firework party that nobody planned but
  everybody enjoys." When it fits the story, this runs better than a
  character performing a gag, because it never stops the story and cannot
  become slapstick. It is one way of being funny, not the required one.
- **We are small and a bit clumsy, and that is lovely.** "We shout, because
  we cannot help it." Let the listener be overwhelmed, or slow, or unable
  to hold still. It is truer than competence and much warmer.
- **Take urgency out of the verbs, not only the nouns.** A wind that
  *lifts* us like two leaves is safer than one that *blasts* us up a
  chimney, even when nothing frightening was named either way. Scan verbs
  for hurry.

**The caution that comes with this section.** The same revision that
produced these moves also grew the opening by 41 per cent and pushed a
segment to within ten words of its ceiling. Warmth is not licence. So:

- Every one of these moves must fit inside the existing ceiling. If adding
  warmth pushes a segment over, the warmth was not free and something else
  must go.
- **Never buy warmth with an abstraction.** "A song that has no words but
  says everything", "the way you mean it when you thank a friend for
  something they didn't have to give" — these are adult reflections about
  the moment rather than the moment itself. A five-year-old hears fog. Say
  the concrete thing and stop.
- **No compound similes at 3-6** — one comparison, one clause. "As if a cat
  were the size of a barn and had swallowed a small engine" is two ideas
  stacked behind a subjunctive; by ear, a young child loses it. But do not
  confuse a compound simile with a **list of simple ones**, which young
  children love: "thick as soup, as porridge, as the jam at the bottom of
  the jar." A run of plain likenesses, each one whole on its own, is
  charming and time-honoured; a single comparison with clauses hanging off
  it is fog. The test is whether each piece stands alone.
- **Do not narrate the meaning of the ending.** The pebble in the pocket
  already says it.

## 7. One motif, and a rhythm that moves

**Motif.** Growing one central image usually beats inventing a fresh one
each paragraph: the mountain has a sleepy song → the song gets louder → the
mountain sings out loud. A phrase that comes back is a gift, most of all to
younger listeners. Do not let it become a chant unless the story wants one.

**Rhythm.** Let sentence length rise and fall — the point is *alternation*,
not any particular count. A long sentence that carries a picture, then a
short one that lands it. Read the segment aloud in your head: if several
sentences in a row have the same shape and length, the passage hums flatly
and a listener drifts. Break the pattern where you hear that. Short
sentences are for the moment something happens.

Still speakable throughout: no ellipses, no asterisks, no parentheses, no
emojis, no stage directions. It will be spoken.

## 7b. The sound of it

This story reaches the child as sound. Write for the ear, not the page:

- **Names should be fun to say.** "Bloop." "Ember." A name a child will
  repeat out loud is worth ten described characters. Test a name by saying
  it three times; if it is a pleasure, keep it.
- **Sound-words get their own sentences.** "Rumble. Rumble." "Bloop." Set
  them alone so a reader lands on them; buried mid-sentence they die.
- **Read the segment aloud in your head, at reading-to-a-child pace.**
  Anywhere your tongue trips — consonant pile-ups, near-rhymes you did not
  intend, two similar names in one breath — rewrite the line.
- **A little music, on purpose, in small doses.** A run of matched sounds
  ("slow gold squiggles") or a rocking repetition can be lovely once or
  twice a story. More than that turns to sing-song, unless the story is for
  ages 3-4, where sing-song is the point.
- **Words the child will taste.** Now and then choose the word that is a
  small pleasure to say — "squiggle", "plop", "burrow" — over its plainer
  cousin. Never at the cost of clarity.

## 7c. Break the segment into paragraphs

Return each segment as **several short paragraphs, one beat each**, with a
blank line between them — never one unbroken block. This is not cosmetic.
The story is narrated one audio clip per paragraph, so a single-paragraph
segment becomes one long clip a child cannot pause and resume inside, and
any glitch in narration takes out the whole segment instead of one
paragraph.

- A new paragraph starts on a new beat: a change of place or action, a turn
  in the conversation, a shift in what we notice. When in doubt, break more
  rather than less.
- A **sound-word or a short line of dialogue set on its own** (see §7b) is
  its own paragraph — that is what lets the narrator land on it.
- Rough aim by age, a lean and not a quota: 3-4 two to four short
  paragraphs; 5-6 four to seven; 7-8 six to ten; 9-12 eight or more. A
  segment that is one paragraph is almost always a mistake at any age above
  3-4.
- Use a real blank line between paragraphs (one line break, not a run of
  them). No other markup: no headings, no bullet lists, no asterisks — it is
  spoken.

## 8. Length — a ceiling, never a target

**Padding is a failure, not a shortfall.** If a beat is done in 90 words,
it is done. Never add description, a joke, or an extra image to reach a
number. Short and moving beats long and pretty, every time.

## 9. Choice moments

- **Usually a character asks the question, in their own voice** — the
  companion turns and asks us: "Two ways," says Ember. "Beside the hot
  river, or up the stairs? Which one?" Occasionally the moment itself poses
  the choice; a narrator announcing it is the weakest of the three.
- We can ask first, so the question is handed over rather than declared.
- Each option carries a short **say-word** the child can echo; the two
  should not sound alike.
- Options differ **in kind** (daring vs. clever, close vs. far), not in
  size of the same thing.
- **The consequence lands in the body**: not "we stay" but "we hold our
  breath and stay".

## 10. Endings

Warm, fully resolved, no cliffhangers, on every branch. Something small
comes home with us — an object, a seed, a name, a promise. The last line
usually belongs to us or to our companion rather than to a summary.

## 11. Safety, competence, and truth

- Nothing on the topic's avoid list, on any path. Cartoon-safe: no injury
  detail, no menace, no peril framed as real.
- **When the story visits a real place a child could actually stand in —
  a volcano rim, a hot spring, a geyser field, deep water, a beehive — the
  characters should show, once and lightly, that they know how to be there
  safely.** Not a warning, not alarm: competence. "Ember knows the good
  ledge. We stand behind the warm rock, where the sparks cannot reach, and
  watch." One clause of positioning is enough, and it usually *improves* the
  beat by giving us a vantage point and the guide a job. Skip it where the
  setting is plainly impossible (inside a whale, on Saturn) — imitation is
  not a risk there.
- Never say a thing could hurt us, never let a character be scolded for
  getting close, never let the safety beat carry tension. Competence reads
  as confidence; warnings read as fear.
- Facts must be true. Use the topic's vocabulary naturally; each new word
  gets a picture built around it.
- Feelings: shown through body and behaviour. At 3-6 a feeling may be named
  plainly **when paired with shown behaviour** ("He is a little scared. He
  swims closer anyway"). Naming becomes rarer as age rises.
- Never use: assessment, mastery, milestone, delayed, gifted, grade level.
  ("score", "ahead of" and "behind" are **not** banned — they are almost always
  literal in a story, and banning them was the single biggest source of false
  blocks. Write them when they are literal.)

## 12. Before you return the segment

Judgment questions, not a checklist to satisfy mechanically:

1. Read it aloud in your head. Where would this child's attention slip?
2. Is anything here because a length was expected? Cut it.
3. Does any description fail all four tests — invite, involve, advance,
   ignite? Is any image weak enough to be worse than the plain true thing?
4. Is the companion doing something, or only being present?
5. Does the rhythm rise and fall, or hum at one length?
6. Is a rule showing through — dialogue openings, food similes, the same
   joke shape as the last segment? Break it.
7. Is every object I introduced used, here or later?
8. If this is a choice: does a character ask it, do the say-words differ,
   does the consequence land in the body?
9. If we are somewhere real and warm or deep, did we show we know where to
   stand — lightly, without fear?
10. Is it broken into short paragraphs, one beat each, with a blank line
    between — not one block? (§7c)
11. Fit for **this age**, not for children in general — the right register,
    not the youngest one louder? (§2b) Does every sound-word carry a fact?
