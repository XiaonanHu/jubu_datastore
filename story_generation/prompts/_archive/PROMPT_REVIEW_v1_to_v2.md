# Prompt Review — v1 → v2

> **Kind:** archive · **Status:** historical provenance, not operational.
> Why the v2 writing rules say what they say. The prompt is now at v2.3.

**For:** reviewing the story-writing prompt change before we regenerate any
stories. **Driven by:** a children's-storytelling expert's line-by-line
review of "The Mountain That Breathes" (July 2026), plus the founder's two
observations (abrupt opening, description overload).

Files: `segment_writing_v1.md` (beside this one; what produced the 22
pilot stories) and `../segment_writing_v2.md` (the replacement).

---

## 1. The two root causes

**The opening was abrupt because no rule existed.** v1 said the pattern's
first beat was a "warm opening in a familiar place", which the writer
satisfied with a door. Nothing required establishing who "we" are, where we
are standing, or why we would open anything. **Not intentional — a hole.**

**The description overload was caused by the prompt itself.** v1 set
"150-200 words per segment" *and* "vivid and concrete always", with no test
for whether a description earns its place. A writer with words to fill and
no cut rule decorates. Every phrase the expert marked "irrelevant" is
padding manufactured to hit my word count. This is the single most
important change in v2: **length is now a ceiling, and padding is defined
as a failure.**

## 2. Every expert comment, and the rule it produced

| Expert comment (on the volcano story) | New rule in v2 |
|---|---|
| "We could just say a tunnel appears… make it more vivid, personal, bring people in. Such as *A tunnel appears, as if it's just waiting for us*." | §4 the **job test**: a description must *invite* (reach toward us), *involve* (felt in the body), or *advance* (used later). Otherwise cut. |
| "Remove *glows orange, as if someone left a nightlight on inside the whole mountain* — not relevant so much." | §4 cut list: colour and light details that touch nothing. |
| "The length of the sentences should vary. Some long sentences and some effective short sentences." | §7 **rhythm rule**: every segment needs at least one 2-5 word sentence and one longer flowing one; never three similar-length sentences in a row. (v1's "mostly under 15 words" caused the flatness.) |
| "*Out from under a rock ledge pops a small lizard* — these descriptions are irrelevant, not related to our experience." | §4 cut list: never describe where a character came from; introduce them by what they do or say **to us**. |
| "Lizard could be the protagonist asking us where should we go." | §9: **a character asks the choice question in their own voice**, never the narrator. |
| "We listen, the lizard listens with us too." / "Lizard said, *listen listen*." | §3: the companion **participates in every beat**, and speaks in short repeated kid-shaped phrases. |
| "Too much irrelevant things" (the squeaky-rumble gag) | §4: **at most one comic bit per segment**, and only if it moves the story. |
| "*One spark lands right on Ember's nose* — very bad, makes the story slow." | Same rule; v1 stacked four gags across two segments (hat falls, hat slides, spark on nose, crossed eyes). |
| "Kids are always interested in food. Slow bubbles look like grandma's thick tomato soup… all things could be related to daily stuff." | §5: **similes must come from the child's own day** — bread, soup, dough, bathwater, a purring cat. Food images preferred. Abstract or adult comparisons banned. |
| "*Suddenly, a huge bubble raise up like a dough puffing up in a warm kitchen*" — marked **good** | §4: **one image per beat, placed on the action**, with a sudden active verb. This sentence is quoted in v2 as the model. |
| "*cozy warm* → bad, too abstract, adult-facing words" | §4 cut list: **adult mood words banned** (cozy, magical, wondrous, serene). Say what the body feels instead. |
| "We hold our breath and stay" (rewriting "We stay") | §9: **choice consequences are embodied** — the branch is felt in the body in its first sentence. |
| "We are very curious. *What* we asked. What should we do now?" | §3: **we speak too** — curiosity is audible, not narrated. |
| "Don't need this" (crystal window, green hillsides) | §4 cut list: no scenery introduced and never used again. |
| "Needs something more vivid, too many irrelevant things" (stairs spiralling) | §4 cut list: **do not narrate travel — arrive.** |
| "sing and erupt" | §6: **one motif, developed** — the mountain's sleepy song grows into the mountain singing out loud, instead of a fresh image every paragraph. |
| Founder: the opening is sudden, what are "we", who is me | §2: the **first 60 words** must establish who we are, where we are, and the pull that takes us in. A door needs a reason to be opened. May open on a spoken line. |
| Founder: too many descriptions, I get lost | §8: length is a **ceiling not a target**; padding is a failure. Plus §3's ratio: roughly half of each segment should be dialogue or physical action. |

## 3. The same opening, under both prompts

**v1 output (what she reviewed) — 108 words to reach the choice, all narration:**

> At the bottom of the tallest mountain, we find a little round door. It is
> warm, like bread fresh from the oven. We push open the door and step
> inside together. A tunnel glows orange, as if someone left a nightlight
> on inside the whole mountain. Then we hear it. Rumble. Rumble. The sound
> rolls under our feet like a giant humming in its sleep. Out from under a
> rock ledge pops a small lizard with pebbly orange skin. It is Ember, the
> lava-lizard, and a tiny hat of grey ash sits on its head. Ember bows so
> low the hat falls off, plops back on upside down, and stays that way…

**v2 rewrite of the same beat — 139 words, primed, dialogue-led:**

> "Do you hear that?"
>
> We are sitting on the warm grass at the bottom of the tallest mountain,
> you and me, when the ground starts to hum. It feels like a cat purring.
> A very big cat.
>
> There is a little round door in the rock. We touch it. It is warm, the
> way the oven door goes warm when bread is baking inside.
>
> We push. The door opens. A tunnel is waiting for us.
>
> "You came! You came!"
>
> A small orange lizard is hopping in front of our feet. Grey ash dusts her
> back like flour on a counter.
>
> "I am Ember," she says. "The mountain is awake today. It is singing. Do
> you want to hear where the song comes from?"
>
> We do.
>
> "Two ways," says Ember. "Beside the hot river, or up the stairs into the
> rumble. Which one?"

What changed, concretely: we are established (sitting on warm grass, you
and me) before any door; the pull arrives *at us* (the hum); the tunnel
*waits for us* instead of glowing decoratively; Ember arrives by speaking,
not by emerging from under a ledge; the hat gag is gone; similes are cat,
oven bread, flour on a counter; sentence lengths swing from three words to
twenty-two; the motif (the mountain's song) is planted in dialogue; and
**Ember asks the choice question**, not the narrator.

## 4. What this does not fix — needs your mother's ruling

Three things I deliberately did not encode, because they are judgment calls
she is better placed to make than I am:

1. **How much rumble-and-repeat is right at 5 versus 6.** Repetition is a
   gift to a listener, but where does it tip into babyish for a six-year-
   old? v2 says "repetition is how a child holds a story together" without
   a limit.
2. **Whether the quiet_hour pattern works at all at this age** — a story
   where almost nothing happens is exactly the register v2's "would a child
   lean in, or drift?" test is hardest on. Her read on "The Night the Bay
   Turned Into a Lamp" would settle it.
3. **Naming a feeling.** v2 keeps the current rule (may name it at 5-6 only
   when paired with shown behaviour). She may want it stricter or looser.

## 5. What to do with the 22 existing stories

They were all written under v1, so they all carry its flaws in some
measure. Recommended, in order:

1. **Rewrite the volcano story under v2 by hand** and put the two versions
   in front of her side by side. If she prefers v2, the prompt is validated
   cheaply, before any bulk work.
2. **Then rewrite the five other review-set stories** (jellyfish,
   dinosaurs, whales & dolphins, rainforests, ants) — the one-per-pattern
   set — so her review session reads v2 prose rather than prose we already
   know is flawed.
3. **Leave the remaining sixteen as v1 artifacts** until the pipeline
   exists; they become the comparison set for the evaluation harness
   (v1 prompt vs. v2 prompt, blind read). That is exactly the harness's
   first real job.

## 5b. What v2.1 changed from v2.0 (founder review)

v2.0 fixed the expert's findings but introduced a new flaw of its own: it
converted craft judgments into absolute rules and numeric quotas — the same
mistake as v1's word count, in a different costume. v2.1 corrects that:

| v2.0 said | v2.1 says | Why |
|---|---|---|
| written throughout for "a five-year-old" | age band and age are **inputs**, with a per-band table (sentences, repetition, voice-vs-narration, length) | the prompt serves 3-4 through 9-10; hardcoding one age was simply a bug |
| "the companion participates in **every** beat… never a decoration" | mostly doing something; quiet beats allowed — "a guide who is silent at the right moment is powerful" | absolutes produce mechanical writing; a companion needs range |
| kid-shaped repeated speech, stated flatly | reach for it often at 3-6, less at 7-8, rarely at 9-10, and not in every line at any age | it is age-dependent and becomes a tic if universal |
| a description must invite, involve, or advance — else cut | fourth test added: **ignite** (stirs a feeling, or makes real knowledge land). "Beauty is not decoration." | good description can simply be beautiful; the real failure is padding or weak writing |
| "cut colour and light details that touch nothing", citing "glows orange, like a nightlight" | "the tunnel glows orange" is **fine**; the weak abstract comparison is the problem — make images specific or let the plain true thing stand | the founder's correction: the colour was not the fault |
| cut list framed as "always cut" | "usual suspects when a segment feels padded" | judgment, not prohibition |
| "at least one sentence of 2-5 words and one longer one per segment" | **alternation**: let length rise and fall; read it aloud and break any flat stretch | a count is exactly the arbitrary rule the founder objected to |
| motif rule stated as a prohibition on new images | motif as a lean, plus permission for a **list of concrete delights** ("as tomato soup, as porridge, as the jam at the bottom of the jar") where it pleases | children enjoy runs of cute concrete things; do not forbid it |
| — | new §0: **formula is a failure mode equal to padding**; if a rule's shape shows through across segments or stories, break it | rules applied evenly become visible and dull |
| — | new §11 clause: **real-place safety as competence** (see below) | founder's imitation-risk question |

## 5c. The imitation question, and how it is handled

The founder asked whether a story that takes us close to a volcano's heart
implies it is safe to get close in real life, and whether a line like "we
find a safe place to watch" would help.

The judgment taken: **yes, but as competence, never as warning** — and only
where the setting is one a child could actually stand in. The distinction
that matters is *imitability*, not scariness:

- **Plainly impossible settings** (inside the mountain, on Saturn, inside a
  whale) carry little imitation risk; a safety beat there would be odd and
  would break the spell.
- **Real, visitable, genuinely hot or deep places** — a volcano rim, a
  geyser field, hot springs, deep water, a beehive — are where a child might
  copy what the story did. Here the characters show, once and lightly, that
  they know where to stand: "Ember knows the good ledge. We stand behind the
  warm rock, where the sparks cannot reach, and watch."

Why competence rather than caution: a warning ("that could burn you")
imports fear into a story built to be fear-free, and it also tells the child
the place is dangerous rather than showing them how it is enjoyed. A guide
who simply knows the good ledge reads as confidence, and it usually
*improves* the beat — it gives us a vantage point and gives the companion a
job. The rule explicitly forbids tension in the safety beat and forbids a
character being scolded for getting close.

**This one should go to the psychologist for confirmation.** It is a
judgment about what a five-year-old generalises from a story, which is
exactly her expertise, and the hot-springs and geyser stories already
written are the concrete cases to test it against.

## 6. Rules now enforceable automatically

Only two things are exact enough to **block** a segment:

- **segment length**: a hard ceiling, with no minimum (padding is the
  failure, so there is nothing to enforce on the short side);
- **safety**: avoid-list terms and the banned evaluation words.

Everything else about craft is a **warning for human eyes**, because a
blocking threshold on a judgment call is how arbitrary rules sneak back in:

- flat rhythm — flag a segment whose sentence lengths vary little (low
  spread, or a run of several near-identical lengths) rather than demanding
  specific short and long sentences;
- abstract mood words (cozy, magical, wondrous, serene) — flag as *probably*
  telling a feeling, since context sometimes justifies them;
- low share of speech or physical action — flag, do not fail;
- an object introduced and never used again — flag;
- a comparison repeated across nearby segments, or a joke of the same shape
  twice — the formula detector.

The workflow doc's Stage 5 gate has been updated to match this split.
